## 2026-05-25 — Vegan / plant-based diet for someone with heart disease

**Context:** A friend with a heart condition is being encouraged to eat "more vegan." What does the evidence say, and what are the trade-offs?

### What the evidence shows (the upside)

The evidence base for plant-forward eating in heart disease is genuinely strong — stronger than for most diet interventions. A 2024 umbrella review pooling 21 studies found vegetarian and vegan diets associated with ~29% lower CVD incidence, ~32% lower ischemic heart disease mortality, and ~41% lower cerebrovascular disease risk versus omnivorous diets, along with improvements in LDL-C, blood pressure, triglycerides, CRP (inflammation), and BMI. The mechanisms make sense — less saturated fat, more fiber, more polyphenols, more potassium, less sodium when home-cooked.

The strongest single piece of evidence is the **Ornish Lifestyle Heart Trial**. It's the only program shown in randomized trials to actually *regress* coronary atherosclerosis (measured by angiography), not just slow it. The intervention is intensive — a very-low-fat (~10%) whole-food plant-based diet, exercise, stress management, and group support — but it works. Medicare added it as a covered Intensive Cardiac Rehabilitation benefit in 2010, which is unusual for a lifestyle program.

The other heavyweight is the **Mediterranean diet** (PREDIMED trial and follow-ups). It's not strictly vegan — it includes fish, olive oil, some dairy, occasional poultry — but it's plant-forward. PREDIMED was stopped early because cardiovascular events dropped so much in the Mediterranean arms vs. the low-fat control. The mortality picture in re-analysis is more nuanced (clearer for stroke than for total mortality), but the overall pattern is favorable and the diet is much easier to sustain than strict Ornish.

### What to watch out for (the downside)

Strict veganism carries some specific nutrient risks that matter for heart health:

- **Vitamin B12** — essentially absent from plants; supplementation is non-negotiable. Untreated B12 deficiency raises homocysteine, which is itself a cardiovascular risk factor.
- **Omega-3 EPA/DHA** — vegans get much less. ALA from flax/chia/walnuts converts to EPA/DHA but the conversion is <10% efficient. Algae-based omega-3 supplement (250–500 mg EPA+DHA daily) is the standard recommendation. Relevant because EPA/DHA are independently cardioprotective.
- **Vitamin D, iodine, iron, zinc, selenium, calcium** — all need attention but are manageable with planning.
- **Ultra-processed "vegan" foods** — a 2025 study highlighted that not all plant-based eating is healthy; vegan junk food (refined grains, plant-based meats high in sodium, sugary plant milks) can actually raise CVD risk. The wins come from whole foods, not the vegan label.

### The pragmatic read

For a friend who already has a heart condition, the *direction* is well-supported. The two main evidence-backed options are:

1. **Whole-food plant-based / Ornish-style** — strongest evidence for actual disease regression. Requires real commitment and supplementation (B12, algae omega-3).
2. **Mediterranean** — strongest RCT evidence for event reduction, much easier to sustain, retains fish (good for EPA/DHA) and small amounts of dairy/poultry.

A reasonable middle path is "mostly plants, with fish a few times a week, minimal red meat" — which captures most of the benefit of either and avoids the supplementation gymnastics. If his cardiologist hasn't already named a specific framework (Ornish, Mediterranean, DASH), worth asking — they'll have a view.

## 2026-05-25 — Why were cardiovascular outcomes worse in the 60s/70s?

**Context:** Follow-up from the "were diets healthier in the 60s/70s?" question. US coronary heart disease mortality peaked around 1968 and has been falling ever since. Why?

### The headline answer

It's roughly half-and-half: **about 44% of the decline came from risk-factor improvements (smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol), and about 47% from medical treatment advances (statins, stents, thrombolytics, beta blockers, ACE inhibitors).** That's from the well-known IMPACT model applied to US data from 1980 to 2000. Both halves are huge — neither one alone would have produced the drop.

### The risk-factor half

Smoking is the single biggest behavioral story. Adult smoking prevalence was **42% in 1965** (right after the 1964 Surgeon General's report) and is around **12% today**. That alone removes an enormous CVD driver. Population cholesterol levels fell substantially even before statins arrived in 1987 (partly from diet shifts away from trans fats and partly from awareness), and hypertension treatment got dramatically better starting in the 70s. Notably, ~79% of the risk-factor gains came from healthier people getting healthier (primary prevention), not from sick people improving.

### The treatment half

In 1968 you basically couldn't *treat* a heart attack much. No statins (1987). No PCI/angioplasty until the late 70s. No coronary stents until the late 80s. No thrombolytics in routine use until the 80s. No widespread beta blocker or ACE inhibitor use post-MI until later still. CABG existed but was new. Acute coronary syndromes that today get rapidly opened up and discharged on optimal medical therapy were, in the 60s, much more often fatal or chronically debilitating.

### Where does diet fit?

Diet is woven through both halves but isn't the dominant single factor. The 60s/70s American middle-class diet *was* heavy in saturated fat (whole milk, butter, beef, pork, cheese) and, importantly, in **trans fats** from margarine, Crisco, and packaged baked goods. Trans fats are now understood to be substantially worse for cardiovascular risk than the saturated fats they replaced — a genuine public-health own-goal that took decades to undo. The diet-heart hypothesis from that era (saturated fat → cholesterol → CVD) is now considered partially true but oversimplified; recent re-analyses of unpublished 60s/70s trial data showed weaker effects than the prevailing narrative claimed at the time.

The cleanest summary: **smoking decline + better acute and chronic cardiac medicine** explain most of the gain. Diet improvements (especially trans-fat reduction) contributed, but the headline story is tobacco and treatment.
